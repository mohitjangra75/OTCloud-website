Developmental Differences Can Appear in Many Ways

Development is not limited to speech or academic learning. It includes how a child communicates, plays, moves, responds to sensory information, manages emotions, learns new skills and participates in everyday activities.

Explore the areas below to understand commonly observed concerns. These examples are not diagnostic criteria and may have different causes in different children.

Speech and Communication

Children develop communication through sounds, gestures, words, sentences and their ability to understand others.

Parents may notice

  • Limited use of sounds, gestures or spoken words
  • Difficulty expressing needs, choices or feelings
  • Difficulty understanding questions or instructions
  • Repeating words or phrases without using them meaningfully
  • Speech that is difficult for familiar or unfamiliar people to understand
  • Limited back-and-forth conversation
  • Loss of words or communication skills previously used
Explore Speech and Communication Concerns →
Social Interaction

Social development includes noticing other people, sharing attention, participating in play and responding during social interaction.

Parents may notice

  • Inconsistent response to name
  • Limited sharing of interests, enjoyment or attention with others
  • Difficulty initiating or maintaining interaction
  • Limited use or understanding of facial expressions and gestures
  • Preference for solitary play most of the time
  • Difficulty joining peer play or understanding social boundaries
  • Limited pretend, cooperative or imaginative play
Explore Social Interaction Concerns →
Sensory Processing

Sensory processing refers to how the nervous system notices, organises and responds to information from touch, sound, movement, vision, smell, taste and the body.

Parents may notice

  • Strong reactions to sounds, touch, textures, lights, smells or crowds
  • Avoidance of grooming, haircuts, nail cutting or certain clothing
  • Extreme food preferences related to texture, smell or temperature
  • Frequent movement-seeking, spinning, jumping or crashing
  • Fear or avoidance of swings, stairs, playground equipment or movement
  • Unusual response to pain, temperature or physical contact
  • Difficulty remaining regulated in busy environments
Explore Sensory Processing Concerns →
Attention and Regulation

Attention and self-regulation help a child remain engaged, shift between activities and manage their level of alertness.

Parents may notice

  • Difficulty remaining engaged in age-appropriate activities
  • Frequently leaving tasks before completion
  • Difficulty following routines or multi-step instructions
  • Constant movement that interferes with participation
  • Becoming overwhelmed easily in busy environments
  • Difficulty waiting, taking turns or controlling impulses
  • Significant difficulty shifting from one activity to another
Explore Attention and Regulation Concerns →
Behaviour and Emotional Regulation

Behaviour is a form of communication. Persistent behavioural difficulties may reflect challenges with communication, sensory regulation, flexibility, emotional regulation or environmental demands.

Parents may notice

  • Frequent or intense meltdowns
  • Aggression towards self, other people or objects
  • Significant distress during changes or transitions
  • Very rigid routines or repetitive patterns of behaviour
  • Difficulty recovering after frustration
  • Extreme fears, worry or avoidance that affects participation
  • Behaviour that interferes with home, school or community routines
Explore Behaviour and Emotional Concerns →
Learning and School Participation

Learning involves attention, memory, problem-solving, language, visual processing and the ability to participate in classroom routines.

Parents may notice

  • Difficulty understanding early concepts
  • Difficulty remembering or following instructions
  • Challenges with reading, writing, spelling or mathematics
  • Poor handwriting readiness, pencil control or written output
  • Difficulty completing classroom or homework tasks
  • Avoidance of academic activities
  • A noticeable difference between the child's effort and performance
Explore Learning and School Concerns →
Movement and Coordination

Movement development includes posture, balance, strength, coordination, mobility and hand function.

Parents may notice

  • Delayed sitting, standing, walking or other movement skills
  • Frequent falling, clumsiness or poor balance
  • Difficulty running, jumping, climbing or using stairs
  • Poor posture or reduced endurance during sitting and play
  • Difficulty using both sides of the body together
  • Challenges with pencil control, scissors, buttons or small objects
  • Avoidance of sports, playground or movement-based activities
Explore Movement and Coordination Concerns →
Daily Living Skills

Daily living skills help children participate independently in their home, school and community routines.

Parents may notice

  • Difficulty eating independently or using utensils
  • Restricted food choices or difficulty managing food textures
  • Difficulty dressing, fastening buttons or managing footwear
  • Delayed toileting or difficulty recognising body signals
  • Resistance to bathing, brushing teeth, grooming or hair care
  • Difficulty organising belongings or following daily routines
  • Dependence beyond what would usually be expected for the child's developmental level
Explore Daily Living Concerns →

The examples above are general observations and do not confirm a developmental disorder. Similar behaviours may arise from different developmental, medical, sensory, emotional, environmental or learning-related factors. A professional evaluation considers the complete developmental profile of the child.

Age-Wise Development Guide

Developmental milestones are skills that many children acquire within a general age range. They provide useful reference points, but they should not be used as a test or diagnosis. Select your child's age to review skills that may be developing and signs that may be worth discussing with a professional.

Development During the First Six Months

Skills Many Babies Develop

  • Looks towards familiar faces and begins recognising caregivers
  • Smiles or responds during social interaction
  • Reacts to voices and environmental sounds
  • Makes cooing, squealing or other sounds besides crying
  • Takes turns making sounds with a caregiver
  • Tracks nearby people or objects with their eyes
  • Brings hands towards the mouth and reaches for objects
  • Develops improving head control
  • Pushes up through the arms during tummy time
  • Begins rolling or shifting body position

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Baby

  • Does not react to loud or familiar sounds
  • Rarely looks towards faces or moving objects
  • Does not smile or respond socially by approximately four months
  • Appears unusually stiff, floppy or consistently uses one side differently
  • Has persistent difficulty holding the head steady
  • Does not reach for or attempt to hold objects
  • Has persistent feeding, swallowing or weight-gain concerns
  • Loses a skill that was previously demonstrated

Clinical note: Concerns involving feeding, swallowing, hearing, vision, growth, unusual movements or loss of skills should also be discussed promptly with your child's paediatrician.

Development Between Six and Twelve Months

Skills Many Babies Develop

  • Responds to familiar people and increasingly responds to their name
  • Uses different sounds and repeated babbling
  • Uses gestures such as reaching, showing or waving
  • Enjoys interactive games such as peek-a-boo
  • Looks for an object that has been hidden
  • Transfers objects between hands
  • Sits with increasing stability
  • Moves across the floor by rolling, crawling, scooting or another pattern
  • Pulls towards standing or stands with support
  • Uses fingers to pick up small pieces of food or objects

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Baby

  • Rarely responds to sounds, voices or their name
  • Uses very few sounds, facial expressions or gestures
  • Shows limited interest in interacting with familiar people
  • Does not reach for, hold or explore objects
  • Has difficulty sitting with support by the later part of this period
  • Does not bear weight through the legs when supported
  • Shows a persistent difference between the two sides of the body
  • Loses social, communication or movement skills

Clinical note: Children do not all crawl in the same way, and some may skip conventional crawling. The overall quality and progression of movement are more informative than one isolated milestone.

Development Between One and Two Years

Skills Many Children Develop

  • Uses gestures such as pointing, waving and showing objects
  • Understands familiar words and simple instructions
  • Uses an increasing number of meaningful words
  • Begins combining two words towards the later part of this period
  • Points to familiar objects or body parts when asked
  • Imitates actions performed by adults or other children
  • Uses objects functionally and begins simple pretend play
  • Walks independently and begins attempting to run
  • Uses both hands during play and daily activities
  • Begins using a spoon, cup or other everyday objects with assistance

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Child

  • Rarely uses gestures to request, show or share interest
  • Does not use meaningful words or communication consistently
  • Has difficulty understanding familiar instructions
  • Shows limited response to name or social interaction
  • Does not imitate actions or engage in simple functional play
  • Is not walking independently by approximately 18 months
  • Has persistent difficulty chewing, swallowing or accepting food textures
  • Uses one side of the body considerably more than the other
  • Loses previously used words, gestures, play or movement skills

Clinical note: Loss of previously acquired language, social or movement skills should not be managed through a wait-and-see approach. Discuss regression promptly with a paediatrician or developmental professional.

Development Between Two and Three Years

Skills Many Children Develop

  • Combines words and gradually uses short sentences
  • Uses language, gestures or other communication to express needs
  • Understands familiar questions and follows simple instructions
  • Identifies familiar objects, pictures and body parts
  • Engages in simple pretend play
  • Plays beside other children and begins joining them
  • Runs, kicks a ball and manages steps with increasing control
  • Uses crayons, blocks, puzzles and simple tools
  • Begins participating in dressing, feeding and toileting routines
  • Follows familiar routines with adult support

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Child

  • Is not using words, gestures or another effective means to communicate
  • Is not beginning to combine words by around two years
  • Has substantial difficulty understanding simple language
  • Rarely shares attention, shows objects or seeks interaction
  • Shows very limited functional or pretend play
  • Frequently falls or has significant difficulty walking and running
  • Has marked sensory reactions that interfere with daily routines
  • Has severe or frequent dysregulation that limits participation
  • Loses any previously acquired skill

Clinical note: At two years, communication involves more than the total number of words. Professionals also consider gesture use, understanding, social communication, imitation, play and whether the child is beginning to combine words meaningfully.

Development Between Three and Five Years

Skills Many Children Develop

  • Communicates using increasingly detailed sentences
  • Participates in back-and-forth conversation
  • Understands and answers familiar questions
  • Tells simple events or stories in sequence
  • Engages in imaginative and cooperative play
  • Notices other children and joins group activities
  • Follows familiar rules and routines with support
  • Runs, jumps, climbs and catches or throws a ball with improving control
  • Uses crayons, scissors and simple construction materials
  • Participates more independently in dressing, eating and toileting
  • Develops early concepts such as colours, shapes, counting and matching
  • Attends to short adult-led learning activities

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Child

  • Is difficult to understand most of the time
  • Has difficulty forming sentences or participating in conversation
  • Has substantial difficulty understanding age-appropriate instructions
  • Shows very limited pretend, interactive or peer play
  • Has extreme difficulty separating, adapting to routines or managing emotions
  • Cannot participate safely in common movement activities
  • Has significant difficulty using crayons, utensils or everyday tools
  • Remains highly dependent in age-appropriate self-care routines
  • Shows persistent repetitive or sensory behaviours that restrict participation
  • Loses skills or becomes increasingly socially withdrawn

Clinical note: Speech clarity, social participation, attention and independence develop gradually. Concern becomes more significant when a difficulty is persistent, occurs across environments or interferes with the child's everyday participation.

Development Between Five and Seven Years

Functional Skills Many Children Develop

  • Communicates ideas, experiences and needs in organised sentences
  • Follows classroom instructions and familiar multi-step routines
  • Participates in conversations and cooperative group activities
  • Develops foundational reading, spelling, writing and number skills
  • Sustains attention during age-appropriate learning activities
  • Copies from a board or book with increasing accuracy
  • Uses pencils, scissors and classroom tools more efficiently
  • Runs, jumps, balances and participates in playground activities
  • Dresses, eats, toilets and manages basic belongings with growing independence
  • Understands basic rules, turn-taking and expected social boundaries
  • Manages minor changes and frustration with decreasing adult assistance

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Child

  • Has persistent difficulty understanding or expressing ideas
  • Struggles significantly with early reading, writing or number concepts
  • Cannot maintain attention sufficiently to participate in classroom tasks
  • Has marked handwriting, fine-motor or visual-motor difficulties
  • Frequently avoids physical activity because of balance or coordination challenges
  • Has difficulty making or maintaining peer relationships
  • Experiences frequent emotional or behavioural dysregulation
  • Remains substantially dependent in everyday self-care
  • Shows a significant difference between ability and school performance
  • Experiences challenges across home, school and community environments

Clinical note: School-age concerns should be understood in relation to classroom expectations, teaching opportunities, language exposure, emotional wellbeing, sensory needs and the child's overall developmental profile.

Development During the School Years

Functional Skills Commonly Expected to Progress

  • Organising and completing increasingly complex schoolwork
  • Maintaining attention and shifting between different tasks
  • Understanding abstract language, humour and social expectations
  • Planning, problem-solving and monitoring work
  • Developing fluent reading, written expression and mathematical reasoning
  • Managing friendships, group work and social conflict
  • Coordinating the body for sports and recreational activities
  • Developing efficient handwriting and keyboard use
  • Managing personal care, belongings, schedules and responsibilities
  • Regulating emotions and asking for assistance appropriately

Consider Seeking Guidance If Your Child

  • Consistently performs below their apparent ability despite appropriate teaching
  • Requires excessive support to begin, organise or complete routine work
  • Has persistent reading, writing, spelling or mathematics difficulties
  • Avoids handwriting, sports, social situations or daily activities
  • Has significant difficulty understanding social cues or maintaining friendships
  • Experiences anxiety, frustration or emotional dysregulation that affects participation
  • Has sensory sensitivities that interfere with school or community life
  • Is frequently described as careless, unmotivated or disruptive despite genuine effort
  • Has difficulty managing age-appropriate independence
  • Shows a new or progressive loss of function

Clinical note: For older children, concerns are often best understood through information from the child, parents, teachers and relevant professionals rather than through a milestone checklist alone.

When Should Parents Seek Professional Guidance?

A single isolated behaviour does not necessarily indicate a developmental concern. Consider seeking professional guidance when a difference is persistent, appears in more than one environment or begins to interfere with the child's learning, relationships, independence or everyday participation.

Parents know their child best. If you remain concerned, it is appropriate to ask for professional guidance even when other people suggest waiting.

Professional guidance may be helpful when

  • A previously acquired skill has been lost
  • Communication is limited or difficult to understand
  • The child does not consistently respond or engage socially
  • Learning or classroom participation is significantly affected
  • Sensory responses regularly interfere with daily life
  • Movement, balance or hand skills restrict participation
  • Behavioural or emotional dysregulation is frequent or intense
  • Feeding, dressing, toileting or grooming remain significantly difficult
  • The child's teacher, doctor or another professional has expressed concern
  • The child appears frustrated by tasks that peers manage more easily
  • The same concern is consistently observed at home and school
  • Development does not appear to be progressing as expected

Frequently Asked Questions

My two-year-old is not talking yet. Is this normal?

Language development varies between children. By around two years, many children use meaningful words, gestures and are beginning to combine two words. Professionals also consider how well the child understands language, uses gestures, shares attention, imitates actions and communicates needs.

Consider seeking guidance if your child is not using words, gestures or another consistent means of communication; has difficulty understanding familiar language; or has lost words that were previously used. A speech and language assessment may include receptive language, expressive language, speech production, social communication, play and oral-motor or feeding abilities when relevant. Hearing should also be considered when speech and language development is delayed.

Why does my child walk on their toes?

Occasional toe walking can occur while young children are learning to walk. Persistent toe walking may be associated with habit, tightness in the calf muscles, reduced ankle movement, sensory preferences, motor-control differences or certain neurological and developmental conditions.

Evaluation may be helpful when toe walking is frequent, continues beyond the early walking period, occurs mainly on one side, causes pain or falling, limits activity or is accompanied by stiffness, weakness or loss of skills. Depending on the presentation, guidance may be required from a paediatrician, paediatric physiotherapist, occupational therapist, orthopaedic specialist or neurologist.

How can I tell the difference between a tantrum and a meltdown?

A tantrum is commonly related to frustration, wanting something or difficulty accepting a limit. The child may remain aware of the response of people around them.

A meltdown is generally an involuntary state of emotional or sensory overload in which the child temporarily loses the ability to regulate effectively. Reasoning, punishment or lengthy explanations are usually not helpful during peak dysregulation. The distinction is not always simple — professionals consider what happened before the episode, the child's communication abilities, sensory environment, demands, duration, recovery and whether the pattern occurs across different settings.

What should I do if my child avoids eye contact?

Eye contact varies between individuals and cultures and should not be considered in isolation. Some children listen or communicate more effectively without maintaining direct eye contact.

Observe the child's broader social communication, including response to name, use of gestures, shared enjoyment, joint attention, imitation, play and ability to engage with familiar people. Avoid repeatedly forcing the child to look directly at you. Seek professional guidance when reduced eye contact occurs alongside other persistent communication, interaction, play or developmental concerns.

Will my child grow out of these behaviours?

Some behaviours reduce naturally as communication, regulation and coping skills develop. Others may continue when the underlying developmental, sensory, communication, learning or emotional need remains unaddressed.

Rather than waiting only for the behaviour to disappear, observe its frequency, intensity, triggers and effect on daily participation. Persistent concerns are better understood through professional assessment than through assumptions about whether the child will outgrow them.

Does an assessment automatically mean my child has a diagnosis?

No. An assessment helps professionals understand the child's strengths, current abilities and areas requiring support. It does not automatically result in a diagnosis or a recommendation for therapy.

Depending on the findings, recommendations may include developmental monitoring, parent guidance, school strategies, therapy, medical evaluation or referral to another qualified professional.

Which professional should assess my child?

The appropriate professional depends on the concern:

  • Occupational Therapist: sensory processing, fine-motor abilities, visual-motor integration, play, regulation and daily living skills
  • Speech and Language Professional: speech, language, social communication and feeding-related communication concerns
  • Child Physiotherapist: posture, mobility, balance, strength and gross-motor development
  • Psychologist: cognition, behaviour, emotional functioning, attention and formal psychological assessment
  • Special Educator: learning profile, academic skills and school participation
  • Developmental Paediatrician: medical and developmental evaluation, diagnosis and coordination of medical referrals

Some children benefit from a multidisciplinary assessment when concerns involve several developmental areas.

Should I wait if my child's school or relatives say everything is normal?

Information from teachers and relatives can be helpful, but parents do not need to ignore a persistent concern. Developmental differences may be more visible in one environment than another.

Record specific examples, note how often they occur and discuss them with a qualified professional. An evaluation may provide reassurance, recommendations for monitoring or early support when required.
