The Assessment

Understand Your Child's Strengths, Needs and the Right Way Forward

When concerns involve communication, behaviour, attention, sensory responses, learning, movement or everyday independence, it can be difficult to know what they mean or what to do next.

A professional developmental assessment brings together parent observations, developmental history, clinical observation and appropriate assessment tools to develop a clearer understanding of your child's abilities and current support needs.

Assessment is not about finding a label. It is about understanding the individual child and identifying the most appropriate next step.

Screening, Assessment and Diagnosis Are Not the Same

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they serve different purposes. Understanding the difference helps families choose the right service and interpret the findings correctly.

Developmental Monitoring

Observing how a child's abilities develop over time across areas such as communication, play, learning, movement, behaviour and daily participation.

It may involve

  • Parent and teacher observations
  • Age-wise milestone checklists
  • Monitoring emerging or changing skills
  • Recording concerns across different environments

Developmental monitoring does not provide a diagnosis or formal risk classification.

Developmental Screening

A brief, structured process used to identify whether a child may be at risk of a developmental difficulty and could benefit from further evaluation.

It may involve

  • A validated screening questionnaire or tool
  • Parent-reported information
  • Brief observation
  • Review of developmental milestones

A screening result does not confirm or rule out a diagnosis.

Clinical Assessment

A more detailed evaluation of specific developmental or functional areas.

It may include

  • Developmental and medical history
  • Parent and teacher information
  • Clinical observation
  • Structured activities
  • Standardised or criterion-referenced tools, when appropriate
  • Professional interpretation of the findings

Helps identify strengths, difficulties and their effect on everyday participation.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Determines whether a child meets recognised criteria for a particular medical, developmental or psychological condition.

The professional required depends on the diagnosis being considered — this may involve a paediatrician, developmental paediatrician, clinical psychologist, neurologist or another appropriately qualified professional.

OTCloud will explain when a diagnostic referral may be appropriate.

A child does not need a confirmed diagnosis before seeking developmental guidance or support.

Why a Professional Assessment Matters

The same observable behaviour may have different underlying contributors in different children. Assessment helps professionals understand the broader developmental and functional picture before making recommendations.

Understanding the Complete Picture

Assessment considers how the child communicates, learns, moves, regulates, interacts and participates across everyday environments — connecting parent concerns with the child's developmental profile rather than examining one behaviour in isolation.

Identifying Meaningful Priorities

Not every difficulty requires the same level or type of intervention. Assessment helps identify which concerns currently have the greatest effect on the child's communication, learning, relationships, emotional wellbeing, physical participation and everyday independence.

Reducing Guesswork

Clinical observation and appropriate assessment tools provide structured information that can reduce reliance on assumptions. The findings may clarify whether the child requires:

  • Developmental monitoring
  • Parent guidance
  • School strategies
  • Therapy
  • Further assessment
  • Medical or specialist referral

Guiding Realistic Recommendations

Recommendations are based on the child's current abilities, participation needs and family priorities. The purpose is to identify an appropriate next step — not to place every child into a standard therapy programme.

Which Type of Assessment May Be Appropriate?

Every child does not need a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. The type and depth of evaluation should match the concern, age, history and purpose of referral.

Initial Developmental Consultation

A structured conversation with a professional to understand:

  • The parent's primary concerns
  • Developmental and medical history
  • Current abilities
  • Home and school participation
  • Previous assessments or intervention
  • The most appropriate next step

Single-Discipline Assessment

A focused evaluation conducted by one professional when the concern primarily involves one area.

  • Occupational Therapy assessment
  • Speech and Language assessment
  • Child Physiotherapy assessment
  • Psychology assessment
  • Special Education or learning assessment

Multidisciplinary Assessment

A coordinated assessment involving more than one professional when concerns affect several developmental areas — Occupational Therapy, Speech and Language, Child Physiotherapy, Psychology or Special Education, depending on the child's needs.

Progress Reassessment

A structured reassessment completed after an appropriate period of intervention to examine:

  • Changes in functional performance
  • Progress towards agreed goals
  • Emerging concerns
  • Current support needs
  • Whether recommendations or goals should change

We Assess the Child, Not Just the Concern

A diagnosis may provide useful clinical information, but it does not describe the whole child. Our assessment considers abilities, participation, environment, family priorities and the activities that matter in the child's everyday life.

01

Child-Centerd

The child's age, communication style, regulation needs, interests and comfort are considered throughout the process.

02

Function-Focused

We examine how developmental differences affect real-life participation in communication, play, learning, movement, relationships and independence.

03

Strength-Based

Abilities, interests and emerging skills are identified alongside areas requiring support.

04

Family-Informed

Parent observations are an essential part of the assessment and provide information that may not be visible during a single clinic appointment.

Our Six-Step Assessment Process

A structured process designed to provide better understanding while respecting the child's individual pace and participation.

1Initial Consultation — Understanding Your Concerns

We begin by discussing what you have noticed, how long it has been present and how it affects your child at home, school or in the community. We may ask about:

  • Pregnancy and birth history
  • Medical and developmental history
  • Communication and play
  • Movement and sensory responses
  • Behaviour and emotional regulation
  • Learning and school participation
  • Daily living skills
  • Previous assessments or intervention
  • Parent and teacher priorities
2Assessment Planning — Selecting the Relevant Areas

The professional reviews the available information and determines which areas require evaluation. The plan may include:

  • Parent or teacher questionnaires
  • Clinical observation
  • Play-based activities
  • Structured tasks
  • Standardised tools, when appropriate
  • Functional assessment
  • Review of schoolwork, reports or videos
  • Referral to another discipline when required

Every child does not complete every test.

3Child Observation and Assessment — Understanding the Child Through Participation

Your child participates in age-appropriate activities selected according to the assessment purpose. Depending on the concern, the professional may observe:

  • How the child communicates and understands
  • How the child plays and interacts
  • Attention and response to instructions
  • Sensory and emotional regulation
  • Posture, movement and coordination
  • Hand function and tool use
  • Learning and problem-solving
  • Daily living abilities
  • Response to support, demonstration or environmental changes

The pace may be adjusted to support comfort and participation.

4Scoring and Clinical Interpretation — Bringing the Information Together

Assessment results are not interpreted as isolated scores. The professional considers:

  • Parent and teacher information
  • Developmental and medical history
  • Direct clinical observation
  • Structured-task performance
  • Assessment scores, when applicable
  • Functional effect on everyday activities
  • Environmental supports and barriers
  • The child's behaviour and participation during assessment

This information is synthesised to identify patterns of strengths, difficulties and support needs.

5Findings and Parent Consultation — Explaining What the Findings Mean

A feedback consultation is conducted to explain:

  • The child's identified strengths
  • Areas requiring support
  • How the findings relate to parent concerns
  • Functional implications at home or school
  • What the assessment can and cannot conclude
  • Whether further evaluation is recommended
  • Possible next steps

Parents are encouraged to ask questions and clarify anything they do not understand.

6Recommendations and Next Steps — Planning the Most Appropriate Way Forward

Recommendations are individualised and may include:

  • Developmental monitoring
  • Parent guidance
  • Home-based strategies
  • School recommendations
  • Single-discipline therapy
  • Multidisciplinary intervention
  • Further assessment
  • Medical or diagnostic consultation
  • Referral to another qualified professional

Assessment does not automatically mean that therapy will be recommended.

What May Be Evaluated?

Assessment areas are selected according to the child's age, concerns, referral question and professional scope. Every child is not evaluated in every area.

Speech, Language and Communication

May include

  • Understanding spoken language
  • Expressing needs and ideas
  • Vocabulary and sentence development
  • Speech-sound production and clarity
  • Functional use of gestures
  • Social communication
  • Conversation and narrative skills
  • Fluency and voice, when relevant
Social Communication and Play

May include

  • Shared attention
  • Social engagement
  • Imitation
  • Reciprocal interaction
  • Functional and pretend play
  • Cooperative play
  • Understanding social cues
  • Peer participation
Sensory Processing and Regulation

May include responses to

  • Touch
  • Sound
  • Movement
  • Visual information
  • Taste and smell
  • Body-position information
  • Internal body signals
  • Busy or unpredictable environments

Also considers how sensory responses affect daily participation.

Fine-Motor and Visual-Motor Skills

May include

  • Grasp and release
  • Hand strength and dexterity
  • Bilateral hand coordination
  • Manipulation of small objects
  • Eye-hand coordination
  • Drawing and copying
  • Scissor use
  • Pencil control
  • Visual-motor integration
Gross-Motor Development

May include

  • Posture
  • Muscle strength
  • Balance
  • Walking and running
  • Jumping and climbing
  • Ball skills
  • Bilateral coordination
  • Motor planning
  • Endurance and functional mobility
Attention and Executive Functioning

May include

  • Task engagement
  • Sustained attention
  • Shifting attention
  • Impulse control
  • Working memory
  • Planning
  • Organisation
  • Flexibility
  • Task initiation and completion
Learning and School Participation

May include

  • Early learning concepts
  • Reading and written-language skills
  • Spelling and mathematics
  • Classroom participation
  • Following instructions
  • Work pace
  • Academic confidence
  • School routines and use of classroom tools
Behaviour and Emotional Regulation

May include

  • Response to frustration
  • Emotional identification
  • Coping abilities
  • Transitions
  • Flexibility
  • Recovery after dysregulation
  • Possible functions of behaviour
  • Effect of communication, sensory and environmental factors
Feeding and Mealtime Participation

When within scope, may consider

  • Food acceptance
  • Texture preferences
  • Mealtime behaviour
  • Use of utensils
  • Seating and posture
  • Oral-motor abilities
  • Chewing and swallowing concerns
  • Sensory responses to food

Medical, nutritional or swallowing concerns may require referral to an appropriate professional.

Daily Living Skills

May include

  • Dressing
  • Feeding
  • Toileting
  • Bathing and grooming
  • Sleep-related routines
  • Organisation of belongings
  • Following everyday routines
  • Age-appropriate independence
Cognitive and Psychological Functioning

When assessed by a qualified professional

  • Cognitive abilities
  • Memory
  • Processing speed
  • Problem-solving
  • Behavioural profile
  • Emotional functioning
  • Attention-related concerns
  • Adaptive functioning

Formal psychological testing is conducted and interpreted only by appropriately qualified professionals.

Participation Across Environments

The assessment may also examine how the child functions across:

  • Home
  • School
  • Therapy environments
  • Playgrounds
  • Community settings
  • Social and family routines

Difficulties may be present in one environment and not another.

What Will You Receive After the Assessment?

The exact deliverables depend on the type and scope of assessment. They will be explained before the assessment is scheduled.

1

Parent Feedback Consultation

A clear discussion of your child's strengths, areas requiring support, relevant assessment findings, functional implications and recommended next steps.

2

Written Assessment Report

When included in the selected package, the report may contain the reason for referral, relevant developmental history, assessment methods, clinical observations, findings, functional interpretation, and recommendations or suggested referrals where applicable.

3

Prioritised Recommendations

Recommendations are organised according to the child's current needs rather than presented as a long, generic list.

4

Initial Home and School Guidance

Practical initial strategies may be provided to support everyday participation while families consider the next step.

5

Referral Guidance

If additional medical, developmental, psychological, hearing, vision, feeding or educational evaluation is recommended, the reason for referral will be explained.

What Should Parents Expect?

Children may respond differently in a new setting. The assessment process is adapted where clinically appropriate to support comfort, regulation and meaningful participation.

Child-Friendly Environment

We use age-appropriate interaction, play, structured activities and movement breaks according to the child's needs and assessment purpose.

Parent Involvement

Parents may be asked to:

  • Remain present for part or all of the session
  • Complete questionnaires
  • Describe home and school concerns
  • Provide examples of challenging situations
  • Share previous reports or work samples

Whether a parent remains in the room depends on the child's age, comfort and assessment requirements.

Observing Natural Abilities

The professional may allow time for the child to become familiar with the environment before introducing structured activities.

When a Child Cannot Complete a Task

Difficulty completing a task is not treated as failure. The professional may:

  • Modify the presentation
  • Provide demonstration or support
  • Offer a break
  • Gather information through observation
  • Reschedule part of the assessment
  • Document why a task could not be completed

The aim is to obtain meaningful information, not to force performance.

What Should Parents Bring?

  • Previous developmental, medical, therapy or psychological reports
  • School reports, teacher observations or relevant work samples
  • Details of current medication and relevant medical history
  • Hearing, vision, neurological or genetic reports, when applicable
  • A list of the parent's primary concerns
  • Details of previous intervention
  • Glasses, hearing devices, mobility aids or communication supports used by the child
  • Familiar items or snacks when advised by the professional

You do not need to collect every document before contacting us. Our team will explain what is relevant for your child's scheduled assessment.

How Long Does the Assessment Take?

Assessment duration varies according to the child's age, primary concerns, number of areas evaluated, type of assessment, number of professionals involved, the child's comfort and participation, and the need for additional information from school or other professionals.

An initial consultation or focused observation may be completed in approximately 45–60 minutes. A detailed single-discipline or multidisciplinary assessment may require a longer appointment or more than one session. Sessions may be divided to reduce fatigue and obtain a more representative picture.

When Will We Receive the Findings?

Preliminary observations may be discussed during or shortly after the appointment when appropriate. The timeline for a written report depends on the complexity of assessment, number of disciplines involved and whether additional information is required. The expected report and feedback timeline will be confirmed before or immediately after completion of the required assessment components.

Clinical Transparency

Clear communication helps families make informed decisions.

Does every child need every assessment?
No. Assessment components are selected according to the child's concerns, history and clinical need. We do not recommend unrelated assessments or multiple disciplines simply because they are available.
Will my child receive a diagnosis?
Not every assessment is designed to provide a diagnosis. OTCloud professionals assess areas within their respective qualification and scope of practice. When a formal medical, developmental or psychological diagnosis is required, families may be advised to consult an appropriately qualified professional.
Does assessment automatically mean therapy?
No. Recommendations may include monitoring, parent guidance, school strategies, therapy, further assessment or referral. Therapy is recommended only when the findings indicate that intervention may be beneficial.
Will we be told about the cost beforehand?
Yes. The assessment type, expected components, professional involvement, fee and available report format will be explained before confirmation. Additional services will not be added without discussing them with the parent or guardian.
What if the findings are inconclusive?
Some children require additional observation, information from another environment or evaluation by another professional. When the available information is insufficient to reach a clear conclusion, this will be communicated honestly and the next appropriate step will be explained.

You will be informed about what is being assessed, why it is relevant, what the findings indicate and what limitations should be considered.

Assessment by the Appropriate Professional

Depending on the concern, your child may be assessed by one professional or a coordinated multidisciplinary team. Each professional evaluates areas within their qualification and scope of practice.

Assessment FAQs

What if my child is shy or does not interact with the professional?
Children may require time to feel comfortable in a new environment. The professional may begin with familiar, play-based or low-demand activities before introducing structured tasks. If the child's participation is limited, this is documented as part of the assessment, and another observation or appointment may be suggested when required.
Can parents remain in the assessment room?
In many assessments, parents may remain present for some or all of the session. In other situations, observing the child with reduced parent involvement may provide useful information. The decision is based on the child's age, comfort, assessment purpose and professional judgement, and will be discussed with the parent.
Can you review my child's school report or previous assessments?
Yes. Previous assessment reports, school observations and work samples can provide valuable contextual information. They support the current assessment but do not replace direct professional evaluation when a new assessment is required.
How should I explain the visit to my child?
Use simple, neutral language appropriate to the child's age — for example: "We are visiting a Center where you will meet someone, play, talk and try a few activities. They want to understand what feels easy for you and what may need support." Avoid presenting the assessment as an examination that the child must pass.
What happens if my child becomes overwhelmed or has a meltdown?
The professional will prioritise safety and regulation. Demands may be reduced, the environment may be adjusted and the child may be offered time or space to recover. The session may be paused or rescheduled if continuing would not provide meaningful information.
What if my child cannot complete all the activities?
Children are not expected to perform perfectly. Incomplete tasks, avoidance, fatigue, difficulty understanding or need for support may themselves provide clinically relevant information. Additional observation may be recommended when necessary.
Does every child need a multidisciplinary assessment?
No. Many children require assessment by only one discipline. A multidisciplinary assessment may be recommended when concerns affect several developmental areas or when coordinated interpretation would provide meaningful additional information.
Does the assessment confirm a diagnosis?
Not necessarily. The purpose depends on the assessment type and professional involved. Some assessments describe developmental or functional abilities without providing a formal diagnosis. If diagnostic evaluation is required, the appropriate referral will be discussed.
Will therapy be recommended after every assessment?
No. Recommendations are based on the findings. A child may require monitoring, parent guidance, school strategies, therapy, another assessment or medical consultation. Some families may receive reassurance that immediate intervention is not necessary.
How much does an assessment cost?
The fee depends on the assessment type, number of disciplines involved, tools required and whether a written report is included. The applicable fee and assessment components will be explained before booking.
How long does the assessment take?
A focused consultation or observation may take approximately 45–60 minutes. Detailed or multidisciplinary assessments may require longer or multiple appointments. The expected duration will be discussed after reviewing the primary concern.
When will we receive the report?
The timeline depends on the scope and complexity of assessment. The expected report and feedback date will be communicated after the required assessment components are completed.
Can OTCloud communicate with my child's school or doctor?
Yes, when collaboration is clinically relevant and the parent or legal guardian provides appropriate consent. The purpose, information to be shared and any applicable service charges will be explained in advance.
How will progress be reviewed if therapy is recommended?
If intervention begins, functional goals are established according to assessment findings and family priorities. Progress may be reviewed through clinical observations, goal achievement, parent and teacher feedback, daily documentation and reassessment when appropriate.
